## At a glance

| Phase | Duration | The wildcard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quote to scheduling | 1–2 weeks | Material lead times; HOA or design review can add 1–3 weeks |
| Tear-off and deck inspection | 1–2 days | Rotted decking found once the old material is off |
| Underlayment and installation | 1–2 days | Rain or gusts over 25 mph stop work entirely |
| Cleanup and final walkthrough | Half a day | — |

A roof replacement is one of those renovation tasks that sounds straightforward on paper — strip the old, install the new — and then reality reminds you that roofs are built to handle thousands of pounds of snow, hurricane-force winds, and everything in between, so doing it right requires more coordination than a weekend warrior's calendar can easily accommodate.

Here's what the timeline actually looks like.

## The short answer

For an average single-family home with asphalt shingles, a professional crew of four to six people can strip the old roof and install the new one in **one to three days** of active work. That's the physical installation window — the time the crew is up on the roof.

But "one to three days" is only part of the story. The full project, from signing a quote to the final cleanup and inspection, typically spans **two to four weeks**. Here's why.

## Phase 1: The quote to scheduling window (1–2 weeks)

After you get your roofer's quote, there's usually a gap before work starts. This is where most homeowners lose control of the timeline — and it's entirely normal.

The roofer needs to order materials. Asphalt shingles are common enough that a local supplier usually has what you need in stock, but specialty tiles, specific color runs, and certain underlayments can require a few days for delivery. A good roofer orders everything before scheduling the start date so the crew doesn't show up with an incomplete truck.

If your local building department requires a permit (many do for reroofing), the inspection appointment adds another variable. Some municipalities issue permits same-day; others need a week.

**What to do:** Order your materials the day you sign. Ask the roofer, "When can you actually start, and do you have the shingles in stock?" If they need to order materials, that becomes part of the schedule, not an afterthought.

## Phase 2: Tear-off and inspection (1–2 days)

The crew arrives early in the morning — often 7 AM — with a full team, equipment, and a dumpster for the old material. A teardown on an average roof takes most of a day. Here's what happens:

**Day one:** The crew strips the old shingles down to the decking. Ladders go up. Tarps cover any areas that might need protection. They pull up nails and old flashing. By end of day, the roof is just bare wood.

**Day one (afternoon), if all is well:** Some crews proceed directly to installation if the weather holds and the deck looks sound. Others wait until the next morning — either because they're out of shingles for the day or because they want a fresh start.

**The deck inspection:** Once the old material is off, the crew walks every square foot looking for rotted or damaged plywood. On a roof that's six or seven years old, the deck is often fine. On a 20-year-old roof, you might find a few problem areas. This isn't a surprise — it's expected — but it *does* affect the timeline.

> **The hidden delay:** Every board that needs replacing adds a few hours. A handful of boards? No problem. Half the roof? That's an extra day for materials and labor. A good roofer will call you before proceeding and explain the scope.

## Phase 3: Underlayment and installation (1–2 days)

Once the deck is confirmed (or repaired), the installation begins. This is the part everyone sees — the new roof appearing piece by piece.

The crew lays underlayment first, then starter shingles, then field shingles in rows. Flashing goes around chimneys, vents, and valleys. Ridge cap goes on last. For a straightforward roof, this is one to two days of work. For a complex roof with multiple dormers, valleys, and hips, it leans toward two.

**Weather is the wildcard.** It only takes a light drizzle to stop work. Shingles don't adhere properly when wet, and working on a wet roof is dangerous. High winds (gusts over 25 mph) also stop installation because loose shingles become projectiles.

## Phase 4: Cleanup and final walkthrough (half a day)

The crew cleans up — magnetic sweeps for nails (important for your dog's sake), hosing off the lawn and driveway, loading the dumpster, and doing a final walkthrough with the foreman. If you're home, they'll point out what they did and flag anything they noticed.

If an inspection is required, the roofer or you will schedule it separately. Some municipalities do a final inspection; others just require the permit to be pulled. Check what's needed for your area.

## What makes a roof replacement take longer?

Every roof replacement runs into at least one of these timeline extenders:

### Weather windows
Roof work is entirely outdoor work. A three-day job can easily stretch to a week if you hit two rainy days and a windy day in the middle. Smart roofers build a one-day buffer into every schedule for this reason.

### Surprise repairs
As mentioned above, rotted decking is the most common find. Less common but more expensive: damaged fascia or rafter tails, insufficient ventilation that needs correction, or structural issues that only become apparent once you can see the roof assembly properly.

### Special materials
If you're going with slate, tile, or a specific architectural shingle color, material delivery time adds to the project clock. Standard three-tab or basic architectural shingles are usually available within a day or two from any local supplier.

### Permit and inspection delays
Some municipalities are fast; others move at their own pace. If your roofer pulls the permit, they'll usually handle inspection scheduling. But the inspection appointment itself can be days or weeks out depending on the town's workload.

### Access problems
Narrow driveways that block a dumpster, low-hanging tree branches, overhead power lines, or HOA approval requirements all add delay. These are easy to miss when you're looking at a price quote and easy to discover only when the crew tries to set up.

## How to keep your roof replacement on schedule

Here are the things you can control:

**1. Pick the right season.** Spring and early fall are ideal. Summer heat can make shingle installation uncomfortable and sometimes problematic if it's too hot for proper adhesion. Winter is usually off-limits in most climates.

**2. Order materials on signing day.** Don't wait for the roofer to remind you. The single biggest scheduling delay is a crew showing up and discovering they need to wait for materials.

**3. Clear your schedule for a concentrated window.** It's better to have a solid three-day window than to spread things across two weeks. Roofers prefer to finish a job in consecutive days rather than coming back to a partially stripped roof.

**4. Handle your HOA or local board early.** If you live in a community with design review requirements, the approval process can take one to three weeks. Factor that into your timeline from day one.

**5. Ask about the deck condition during the estimate.** A roofer who hasn't lifted the old material is guessing. A roofer who walks the roof and asks about the age of the existing deck gives a more honest answer about what to expect.

## How this fits into a broader renovation timeline

If you're doing a roof replacement alongside other renovation work, timing matters. The roof should generally be one of the first things to go if you're planning a bigger project — water damage from a delayed roof can ruin work you've already done on gutters, siding, or interior ceilings.

It's also worth cross-referencing with your [overall renovation plan](/blog/how-to-plan-a-home-renovation-step-by-step/) to make sure the roof job doesn't clash with other trades. If you've already mapped out a [kitchen renovation](/blog/how-long-does-a-kitchen-renovation-take/) or [bathroom renovation](/blog/how-long-does-a-bathroom-renovation-take/) timeline, the roof should generally go first — you don't want a delayed roof schedule holding up other work.

And because [what you track during a renovation](/blog/what-to-track-during-a-renovation/) has a direct impact on how smoothly the whole project runs, the roof replacement is a good opportunity to establish the habit of dated photos and receipts — especially for the deck condition findings, which matter for insurance and resale later.

## The honest summary

A roof replacement is usually a shorter project than homeowners expect — the actual work on the roof is measured in days, not weeks. The longer clock is the stuff that happens around the work: ordering, scheduling, inspections, and the occasional surprise repair once the old roof comes off.

Budget two to four weeks from quote to done, expect one to three days of active work, and build in a weather buffer if you're planning around it. That's the realistic range for a standard asphalt-shingle roof replacement on an average house.
