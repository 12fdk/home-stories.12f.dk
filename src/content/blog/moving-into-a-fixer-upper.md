You have the keys and the house is yours, and the first thing you notice is that every room has a problem. The bathroom is fine but the tile is dated. The kitchen works but the counter is cracked. The hallway smells faintly of damp and one of the outlets does nothing. You have a list in your head and it is longer than the house, and the hardest part is not the fixing — it is knowing what to do *first*.

A fixer-upper is not a project you do once and leave. It is a house you have to live in while it is being fixed, over weeks and months, while your money is limited and your weekends are finite. The first month is where that reality settles in, and it is also the month with the highest cost of doing things in the wrong order, because the mistakes you make now you will spend later to correct.

Here is the order I would use, and the thinking behind each step.

![Floor plans on a desk with a calculator, tape measure, notebook and pen](/stock/09.png)

## Before you unpack a single box

The first thing you do in a fixer-upper is not a repair. It is a walk through every room with your phone and a notebook, photographing everything.

This sounds unglamorous and it is the most useful hour you will spend in the first month. You are building three things at once:

- **A baseline.** What the house looked like on day one, before you changed anything. When you are deep into the project and the hallway is bare walls, you will not remember what its floor was. The photo does.
- **A damage record.** The hairline crack you did not notice, the water stain you decided was old and fine, the loose outlet — a dated photograph of each, taken before you touched anything, is the difference between "that was there when I arrived" and a dispute you cannot win. It is the same kind of record a [documented renovation trail](/blog/documenting-renovation-for-insurance/) is built on, and you want it from day one, not from the claim.
- **A two-column list.** In one column, what is broken, unsafe, or letting water and heat escape. In the other, what is merely tired. You will look at this list more than any other in the first month, so build it before you start anything, or you will end up doing the work in the order you thought of it, which is rarely the order it should be done.

The second thing to do in that first walk is test the systems, not look at the finishes. Turn the hot water on and wait for it to come up. Check that the heating works, or at least that the boiler lights. Open a tap in each room and watch for a slow drip. Look at the roof line and the gutters from the ground. Smell for damp in the corners and the basement. Stand in each room for a minute and notice which ones you actually want to live in. You are checking whether the *house* works, not whether the *decor* is fine, because a house that does not work is the expensive kind.

## The must-do list, in the order it should be done

Once you have the list, you do the first column in a fixed order, and the order matters because the wrong order is the one that produces the most wasted work. Work from the outside and the structure in, toward the surface:

**1. Structure and moisture.** A crack in the foundation, a sagging beam, a persistent damp patch, a wall that is cold and soft. If it is real, it makes the rest of your work pointless if you do the rest first. This is the category where you call a professional before doing anything, because guessing at structure is the one guess that costs the most to get wrong.

**2. Water.** Every leak, every dripping tap, every pipe wrapped in aluminium foil (that is what the aluminium is for — someone before you found a drip and covered it). Water damage is the most expensive kind because it is invisible until it is not, and by then it has usually spread. Fix the active leaks before you do anything a future drip will ruin.

**3. Electricity.** The breaker box, any outlet that is warm or does nothing, any wiring that is not what it should be. An electrical problem is not a cosmetic problem and not one you defer, because the house you are about to live in is one you will sleep in. In many places an upgrade on an older house needs a licensed electrician to sign off the work — check your local rules rather than assuming you can wire it yourself.

![An electrician working at an open wall socket in a partly renovated room, with new cables visible](/stock/20.png)

**4. Heating and the envelope.** Heating that will not work in winter is a must-do *before* winter, not after you have moved in and found out. The envelope — roof, windows, doors, drains, the places the weather gets in — is the same: a leaking roof is a must-do before you spend a cent on the room under it, because that is the room you will be re-doing.

**5. The things that make it liveable.** Once the house is dry, warm, and electrically safe, the must-do list is mostly done and you can start living in it. The rest of the first column is the practical stuff: a working kitchen you can cook in, a bathroom you can actually use, a door on the outside that locks properly. These are must-do, but they are the last of the must-dos, because they are the things that are cheap to rush only once the expensive-and-irreversible stuff above them is already right.

The reason the order is fixed is that each layer above protects the layer below. You do not tile a bathroom that will be re-opened because of a pipe behind the wall. You do not paint a ceiling that will be re-screeded because of a leak above it. Do the work that can survive the next layer first, and the work that cannot survive it last.

## What you can live with, and what you are allowed to leave

The second column of your list — the tired, the dated, the merely-ugly — is where most people get the first month wrong, because they feel compelled to fix all of it and they do not. A fixer-upper is not a show house. It is a house you are going to live in for a long time, and the sensible way to live in one is to let it stay a work in progress.

This is where the budget question actually lives, and it is worth saying plainly: **a fixer-upper budget is not one number, it is a sequence of smaller decisions.** You do not sit down and decide "this house will cost us €40,000 to fix" — you cannot, because you do not yet know what is behind the walls, and the people who tell you you can are the people who then surprise you. What you can do is price the must-do list from real quotes, hold a separate contingency fund for what will turn out to be there, and keep the can-wait list deliberately small for the first year. The [hidden costs an older home tends to hide](/blog/hidden-costs-older-home/) live mostly in the can-wait list — not because they are expensive, but because they are the ones you did not expect to have to decide about at all.

Living with the can-wait list is not a failure of planning. It is the plan. The kitchen can be dated for six months. The hallway can be bare for a winter. The thing that breaks a fixer-upper project is not that it is unfinished; it is that it is unfinished *and* the must-do list is still open. If the can-wait list is in front of you while the must-do list is still open, you have done the one thing that costs the most — making the house look nicer before the house is safe.

## The two mistakes that are expensive

Everything else in the first month is variation. Two mistakes show up again and again, and they are both about doing the wrong thing before the right thing.

**Cosmetic work before structural work.** This is the one that wastes the most money, because the work you did is real work that is now wasted. The new floor under the leaking pipe. The painted wall over the damp. The finished bathroom behind the pipe that is going to be moved. Every one of these is a version of the same error, and the fix is the order above: the must-do list first, the can-wait list after, and the can-wait list only in the order that does not put it in the way of the must-do list.

![An empty room freshly painted white, with a paint tray and roller left on the floor](/stock/11.png)

**Signing a contractor for the whole project before you have the list.** A contractor who is given a fixer-upper and told "do the house" will give you a number that is not a number — it is a guess about a house he has not opened up. A contractor who is given a written list of specific, dated, photographed problems will give you a number that is a number. The [way to deal with a contractor on a job like this](/blog/managing-contractor-change-orders/) starts with the list, because the list is the contract, and without it you are negotiating against your own guess. If you are doing the must-do work as a sequence of separate jobs — which is usually the right shape for a fixer-upper, because each trade does one specific thing — the written list is what lets you compare one trade's quote against the next one's, and it is what keeps the project from becoming one big unexplained invoice.

Both mistakes are the same mistake: deciding about money before you have the facts about the house. The first month is when you are most tempted to decide, because the house is new and the problems are fresh and every room feels urgent. Resist the urge to decide quickly. The house does not need you to be fast; it needs you to be in the right order.

## A small system that keeps the first month from eating you

You are going to make a lot of small decisions in the first month: a quote, a photo, a receipt, a note about why you chose that pipe. If you keep them in your head, they will be in your head for a year and you will re-litigate the same decision every time you walk past the pipe.

The simplest version of the system is one project, on the device you already carry: every finding photographed and dated, every quote attached, every receipt saved, every decision noted with a reason. You can do this with a notebook, a folder, and a spreadsheet, and if that is your natural system, do that — the point is not the tool, the point is that the information is in one place with a date on it, so the next decision is made against the last one instead of against a vague memory. If you would rather have the photos, the budget, the quotes and the receipts together in one app that exports a PDF when the insurer or the buyer asks, [Home Stories](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6754754960) is the version I ended up reaching for.

The system matters most at the two moments where it is not a convenience but the difference between winning and losing: the warranty argument where the date on the photo is the date in the contract, and the resale conversation where the record of what you did, when, and by whom is the story of the house.

## What the first month should look like by the end of it

By the end of the first month, the must-do list should be mostly done or contracted, the can-wait list should be clearly separated from it, and you should be able to say, honestly, whether the house is safe to live in. That is the whole goal of the first month, and it is a much smaller goal than "the house is fixed," which is a goal that has a one-year or a three-year timeline and is not something you are going to be doing in the first month.

You are not going to finish the fixer-upper in the first month. You are not going to do all of the work, and you are not going to do it in the order the problems appeared. You are going to do the work that protects the work, in the order that protects the work, and you are going to live in the rest of it, and that is the plan. The house is going to take a while. You, and your money, are going to be much better off for the order.
