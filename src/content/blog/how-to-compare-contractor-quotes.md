The in-home estimate has a familiar shape. A pleasant salesperson spends an hour and a half at your kitchen table. There is a tablet, a binder of photos and a story about the company. Then a number appears, it is bigger than you expected, and when you say you are getting other quotes, they excuse themselves to "make a call". They come back with a much better price that is good for today only.

Threads about exactly this have been all over the home improvement forums in the past couple of weeks. In one, the "manager" on the phone turned out to be an AI agent. In another, an installation quote priced a mini-split at roughly twice what the unit sells for online. Older threads tell the same story about door-to-door siding and roofing quotes that drop by thousands the moment the owner says no. The comments mostly say the same three things. Get more quotes. Get them in writing. Never sign at the kitchen table.

That advice is right, but it skips the hard part. Once you have three quotes, how do you actually compare them? They almost never price the same job, and the totals on the last page are the least useful numbers in the whole document.

Here is how to compare contractor quotes so the comparison actually means something.

![Three printed quotes laid side by side on a kitchen table with a highlighter, calculator and tape measure](/stock/34.png)

## Why three quotes almost never describe the same job

Ask three contractors to "redo the bathroom" and you will get three different bathrooms. One includes removing the old tile and taking it away. Another assumes you'll arrange the skip. One prices a mid-range shower valve, one prices "allowance: $400", and one doesn't mention the valve at all. One includes the permit, one says "permits by owner", and one is silent.

None of them is necessarily dishonest. Each contractor filled the gaps in your brief with their own assumptions. Those assumptions are where most of the price difference lives, and later, where most of the extras come from.

So the first job isn't comparing at all. It is making sure there is one job to compare.

## Before you ask: write one scope and send it to everyone

A scope doesn't need to be a technical document. A page is enough, written room by room or trade by trade, and it should say:

- **What is being done.** "Remove existing tile, tub and vanity. Install walk-in shower with glass screen, new vanity, toilet and floor tile."
- **What stays.** "Keep existing window and door. Keep radiator, relocate 30 cm left."
- **Who supplies what.** "Homeowner supplies tile and vanity. Contractor supplies everything else."
- **Known unknowns.** "Subfloor condition unknown. Please price repair per square metre as an option."
- **Your constraints.** "One bathroom in the house. Must be usable again within X weeks."

Send the same page to every contractor and ask them to price against it. Ask them to list anything they would do differently or think you have missed. Good contractors often do, and that is free expertise. If you have already set a number for the project, the [renovation budgeting guide](/blog/how-to-budget-a-home-renovation/) covers how to turn that number into a scope you can actually afford before the quotes come in.

## The line-by-line comparison

When the quotes arrive, resist the last page. Put them side by side in a table, by hand or in a spreadsheet, with one row per line below and one column per contractor. Leave a cell empty when a quote doesn't mention something. Those empty cells are the point of the exercise.

| Line | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Scope | Does it match your page, item by item? What did they add or drop? |
| Materials | Brands and models, or an allowance? Is the allowance realistic for what you want? |
| Labour vs materials | Split out, or one lump sum? A split makes outliers visible. |
| Exclusions | Read every "by others", "not included" and "owner to supply". |
| Permits, disposal, protection | Included, excluded, or not mentioned? |
| Making good | Who repairs the plaster, paint and flooring the work disturbs? |
| Timeline | Start date, duration, and what happens if it slips. |
| Payment schedule | Deposit size, and are stages tied to finished work or to dates? |
| Warranty | On workmanship as well as materials? For how long? |
| Validity | How long is this price good for? |

Three of those lines deserve a closer look.

**Allowances.** An allowance is a placeholder price for something you haven't chosen yet: tile, fixtures, a worktop. A low allowance makes a quote look cheap today and expensive later, when the tile you actually want costs three times the allowance and the difference becomes an extra. Compare allowances against real prices for what you have in mind, not against each other.

**Exclusions.** This is where cheap quotes hide. "Excludes electrical work" in a bathroom quote means someone else has to be hired, scheduled and paid. "Excludes making good" means the hole in the hallway wall is your problem. List every exclusion and put a rough price next to it.

**Payment schedule.** A sensible schedule ties payments to finished stages, so you never pay far ahead of the work. The FTC's consumer guidance warns against paying the full amount up front and notes that some US states cap how big a down payment can be. Check your local rules wherever you live. And how a contractor wants to be paid tells you a lot about how they run their business.

## A worked example: three bathroom quotes, normalised

Say you get three quotes for the bathroom above. The numbers are illustrative, but the pattern is typical.

- **Quote A: $14,800.** Itemised. Includes disposal, permit, making good, and a tile allowance that matches what you want.
- **Quote B: $11,200.** Itemised. Excludes disposal and the permit. Tile allowance is well below what your tile costs. Electrical "by others".
- **Quote C: $19,500, or $15,600 if signed today.** One page and one total. The scope is described in a single paragraph.

B looks like the winner by thousands. Now price what it leaves out. Say a skip and disposal run a few hundred dollars and the permit a few hundred more. The tile gap is around a thousand. An electrician for the fan and lighting, booked separately, costs more than that. Suddenly B sits within touching distance of A. And B still carries the risk of a vague line becoming an extra, which is the scenario the [guide to change orders and scope creep](/blog/managing-contractor-change-orders/) is about.

C you can't normalise at all, because there is nothing to normalise. A single total for a paragraph of scope isn't a quote you can compare. It is a number you're being asked to trust. Ask for a breakdown. If the breakdown never arrives, that is your answer.

The adjusted comparison might still favour B, and that's fine. The goal isn't to prove the expensive quote right. It is to compare totals that describe the same bathroom.

## Reading the sign-today discount

A large price drop offered on the spot, conditional on signing now, tells you one thing for certain: the first number was not the price. Either it was padded to leave room for the "discount", or the company is willing to work for far less than it first asked. Neither is a reason to trust the second number more than the first.

The FTC's guidance on avoiding home improvement scams lists pressure for an immediate decision among its warning signs. So are knocking on doors because they're "in the area" and suggesting a lender they know. It also advises getting multiple written estimates, not automatically choosing the lowest bidder, and asking for an explanation when the estimates differ a lot.

The practical response is simple and doesn't require any confrontation:

> Any price I'm offered goes in writing, with a date it's valid until. If it's only good tonight, it isn't a price I can compare, so I can't accept it.

A contractor who believes their price is fair will write it down and let you compare it. In the US, the FTC also notes you are typically entitled to a written statement of your right to cancel within three business days when you sign in your own home. That is useful to know, but it is a safety net, not a reason to sign under pressure.

## When the cheapest bid is the right one, and when it isn't

After you normalise the quotes, the cheapest one is the right choice when:

- it prices the same scope as the others, with realistic allowances;
- its exclusions are few and you have priced them in;
- the payment schedule follows the work, not the calendar;
- the contractor answered your questions clearly and in writing.

It is the wrong choice when the low total depends on gaps, vague lines or allowances you know you'll blow through. It is also wrong when the start date is suspiciously soon for a busy trade, or when the contractor's answer to "what's not included?" is a shrug.

Whichever bid you choose, the gap between it and the next one is useful information. If the cheaper quote wins by less than your contingency, the [contingency budget guide](/blog/renovation-contingency-budget/) is the way to think about whether that saving is worth the extra risk.

## Questions to ask before you pick

Before you commit, put a short list of questions to your top two in writing and keep the answers:

1. What is not included that I might assume is?
2. Which items are allowances, and what do they buy?
3. What happens to the price if you find something behind the wall?
4. Who will actually be on site, and who do I call when something's wrong?
5. When is each payment due, and what has to be finished first?
6. How long is this quote valid?

The answers matter, and so does how you get them. A contractor who replies clearly and in writing before the job usually communicates the same way during it.

## Keep the comparison where you'll find it again

The comparison is only useful if you can find it later. The scope page, three PDFs, a table of normalised totals and a few emailed answers will matter again in week four, when someone says the shower valve "was never in the quote". Keep them together, dated, attached to the project. A folder and a spreadsheet do the job. If you'd rather keep quotes, notes and the committed amount in one place on your phone, [Home Stories](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6754754960) stores documents and notes against the project and tracks committed money separately from what's been spent.

Once you sign, the winning quote stops being a quote. It becomes committed money, and your budget should show it that way from that day, not when the first invoice lands.

## Wrapping up

Comparing contractor quotes is less about finding the lowest number and more about making three documents describe one job. Write the scope once. Make everyone price it. Put the quotes side by side line by line, and price what's missing. Ask the awkward questions in writing. And let any number that only exists tonight go.

Do that and the decision usually becomes obvious. You end up picking a contractor, not a sales script.
