## Why the default recommendation is stale

Most "best renovation app" lists share the same top entry: HomeZada. That is understandable — it is the most feature-complete home management product, with asset tracking, warranty reminders, document storage and project budgets. For years, that was the right answer.

Two things have changed. The first is how renovations get run: most homeowners now plan on a phone and log from the site, not from a desk with a spreadsheet. The second is the app itself. As of October 2026, the HomeZada Mobile app on the US App Store is at version 2.4.0, and the last release was 27 May 2022 — four years ago. The rating is 2.9 stars across 44 ratings.

Both facts take a minute to check yourself, and that is the point: for a tool you will lean on for the next six months, the last-updated date is not a footnote.

## Two searches, two different problems

People type "HomeZada alternative" for two different reasons, and the honest answer depends on which one it is.

The first: you are planning a project, you read a list of apps, and HomeZada was on top. In that case, what you need is a plan and a budget tool, and this post saves you about half an hour of reading — because the top recommendation on the list you are holding is stale.

The second: you are already mid-renovation, and the tool that was supposed to be your single source of truth has started to feel like an archive you update out of duty. In that case, what you need is a mid-project switch, and the section below is the checklist for that.

Either way, the starting point is the same: know what you actually use it for, before you compare names.

The web product and the phone app have become two products with two different life cycles. If your renovation management happens on a laptop, the web dashboard is still alive and still worth a look. If the work is happening in a half-demolished room, the phone is the interface you will actually use — and on the phone, this product has been standing still since before many of the current round-ups were written.

## What HomeZada is still good at

To be fair: the breadth of HomeZada is real, and it is not only about renovations. It tracks the assets of a whole home, schedules warranty work and maintenance, stores documents, and manages budgets across a property. If you own more than one home, or you want one dashboard for everything about the house rather than one project, that breadth is the reason to keep it — and no phone-first renovation tracker replaces it.

The trade-off is where you do the work. The interface was built around a desktop dashboard: menus, tables, depth. Logging a receipt from the builder's yard, with gloves on, while the contractor walks back to the van, is not what that interface is for. A tool you touch twice a week from a desk is not the same tool as one you need ten times a day on site.

## Why these lists get stale — and how to avoid being next

The same three apps show up in nearly every renovation round-up, in the same order, with a new year in the title. That is not a conspiracy; it is a structural problem with the format. A "best apps" list is written once, and then re-published because the URL ranks. The author stops re-opening the App Store, the ratings stop being re-checked, and the list quietly becomes a snapshot of 2021 wearing a 2026 badge.

The pattern is predictable: the most-recommended tool is usually the one with the oldest last-updated date. That is not a coincidence. The tools that are actively improving move out of the "default" slot the moment someone re-checks, and the ones that stop improving stay, because nothing forces a re-check. So if a recommendation has survived several annual refreshes unchanged, that is itself information.

You can avoid being next in line with a two-minute habit: for any tool you are about to commit to, look up the current version and the last release date, and check the rating count, not just the stars. A high score on 40 ratings is not the same thing as a high score on 40,000. The tool that is still being shipped, with a live rating count, is the one that will still be useful in six months. That is the only check this post is asking you to make beyond reading the rest of it.

## What your renovation actually needs

Lay a renovation out end to end and it is four jobs in sequence: planning it, measuring, designing, and executing. The first three are desk work over a few weeks — a beginner's step-by-step plan covers the shape of them — and for those, the choice of app matters less than the plan itself. A browser tab and a solid [renovation budget template](/blog/renovation-budget-template/) carry the load.

Execution is different. It runs for months, in dusty rooms, and it generates a constant trickle of costs, receipts, decisions and photos. The tool for that phase has one hard requirement: it has to work from a phone, in seconds, while something else is demanding your attention. A live budget split into materials, labour and contractor; a dated record of photos and receipts; a task list that survives the weekend.

If that is your situation, a phone-first tracker is the honest answer. [Home Stories](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6754754960) is built for exactly that phase — it keeps the budget, tasks, dated photos and receipts in one project on your iPhone or iPad, free on the App Store.

## The three options, honestly

**1. Keep the desktop tool.** If your work has not started, or if budgets are the only thing you need to track, the HomeZada web dashboard — or whatever desktop product you already use — will do. The trigger to switch is when you start logging from the site: at that point, the tool furthest from your hand is the tool you stop using.

**2. A spreadsheet or a document workspace.** A spreadsheet is still the best planning tool on the market, and the [how-to-budget guide](/blog/how-to-budget-a-home-renovation/) covers the structure. It fails at execution: it wants two hands, a desk, and a version of the file you believe is current. A document workspace like Notion is more flexible, but you build the structure yourself, and it is organised around documents, not around a live project with a running total. Fine for notes; wrong shape for a budget that moves every day.

**3. A phone-first tracker.** This is the option most "HomeZada alternative" searches are really asking for. The head-to-head against the big two names is in [HomeZada vs Houzz Pro](/blog/homezada-vs-houzz-pro/), and the wider field is covered in the [2026 round-up of home improvement apps](/blog/best-home-improvement-apps/). The deciding question in this category is not features — it is friction. A log that takes seconds, from one screen, with the receipt photo attached, is the one that survives to month six. [Home Stories](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6754754960) is what that looks like in practice: a running total, dated photos and receipts and tasks, all on one project, free to install.

## Switching without losing what you've already logged

If you are mid-project in HomeZada — or in any tool — the switch is an afternoon, not a project:

1. **Inventory what you have.** Budget figures (planned versus spent, by category), receipts, photos, open tasks. Four buckets, usually.
2. **Export what exists.** Check the export options in the current tool. If the export is complete, use it. If it is partial, do not fight it: a one-time manual migration beats a month of half-synced data.
3. **Start the new budget from the real figures.** Not the original plan, not "where I think we are". The budget has to be live, which means today's actuals, by category.
4. **Bring receipts and photos over as you find them.** Not in one epic cleanup — the project is moving, and the cleanup will lose items.
5. **Run both for a week.** Log in the new tool and spot-check against the old one. When the numbers agree, the old tool becomes an archive.

The cost is one afternoon. The alternative is keeping a tool that stopped evolving four years ago, for no reason except that it is familiar.

## What to look for in a replacement, in order

Once you know your phase, the choice narrows fast. The four things worth weighing, roughly in that order:

**1. Friction at the point of use.** How many taps from the lock screen to a logged cost? A tool that needs the app opened, a project selected, a form filled and a button pressed will be used less than one that is a two-tap log with the receipt photo attached. Friction is not a comfort issue; it is the difference between a live record and an archive you catch up on at the weekend.

**2. Where the record lives.** A budget that lives in a desktop dashboard is a budget you update when you sit down. A budget on the phone is a budget that stays true while the work is happening. For execution, this one decides the outcome more than any feature list.

**3. How it handles the messy middle.** Receipts that do not match a category. A cost that is half materials, half labour. A decision that changes a task from next week. Tools differ enormously here, and this is where one stops being a form and starts being a working record.

**4. What it gives back at the end.** A project PDF, an export, a clean summary for the contractor, the insurer or the tax adviser. If the tool's end-of-life is a spreadsheet you hand-assemble from screenshots, it did its job halfway.

Feature count is not on this list on purpose. The tools in this category all have the basics — the question was never what a tool can store, but whether you will actually store it.

## How to choose in ten minutes

- You manage **multiple properties** or whole-home maintenance? The desktop platform is the right shape — breadth is the feature.
- You are **planning only**, with work weeks away? A spreadsheet plus a [printable checklist](/blog/renovation-checklist-printable/) is enough, and you can always start the tracker later.
- You are **in execution**, logging from the site? A phone-first tracker, full stop.
- You are **between the two** — planning now, executing soon? Start the budget in the tracker now, so there is nothing to migrate later.
