Ask a contractor how long a kitchen renovation takes and you'll get a number. Ask them how long it took *last* time and they'll tell you how that number drifted.

The honest answer is a range, and the range is determined by what happens behind the cabinets, not what you picked out at the showroom. You can spend three weekends poring over tile samples, but the thing that will actually determine whether your project finishes in six weeks or sixteen is whether they find water damage behind the sink once the old cabinet comes off the wall.

Here's what those ranges look like, what the time is actually spent on, and what makes any given project run long.

## The two most common ranges

**A straightforward refresh — four to eight weeks on site.** This is keeping the same layout: new cabinets, new counters, new flooring, the sink stays where it is, the electrical stays in roughly the same spots. The trades are fairly sequential — demo, then electrical and plumbing rough-in, then cabinets and counters, then flooring, then paint and trim. Nothing moves; nothing surprises.

**A full gut renovation — ten to sixteen weeks on site.** This is knocking out walls, moving plumbing and electrical, getting a new island into the room, changing the floor plan. Now you're looking at structural work, new inspections, a different sequence of trades, and more opportunities for things to go sideways once the demo starts.

Neither of these numbers includes the design and permitting phase that happens *before* the first hammer swings. Add four to eight weeks for that — your local building office, drawing up the plans, getting approved, and ordering the cabinets. Most renovators under-count this phase dramatically because it feels like "just planning" rather than actual work. It isn't. It's the work that determines whether everything else proceeds on schedule.

## What the weeks are actually spent on

A kitchen renovation timeline isn't one continuous block of hammering and screwing. It's a chain of phases, some fast and loud, some slow and invisible, and the gaps between them are where projects lose months. What follows is the shape of those phases and why each one takes as long as it does; if you'd rather see a schedule laid out week by week, [a realistic kitchen renovation timeline](/blog/kitchen-renovation-timeline/) works through one as an example.

**Weeks 1–3: demolition, rough-in, inspections.** This is the loud, messy phase. Old cabinets come down, drywall gets torn open, and the new electrical and plumbing lines go in behind the walls. Once the rough work is done, an inspector needs to sign off before the walls can close up. If everything passes — and a lot of projects don't the first time — you're looking at three to five days of actual work spread across these weeks. The time is spent waiting.

**Weeks 4–8: cabinets, counters, tile, finishes.** The visible transformation happens here. But this is also the phase dominated by cabinet delivery. If you ordered custom cabinets during the design phase, they should be arriving now. If you haven't ordered yet, you're looking at an eight-to-twelve-week wait, which means your partially demolished kitchen sits idle while you wait. Countertop fabrication adds another couple of weeks — the template happens after cabinets are in, then fabrication, then install.

**Weeks 9–12: flooring, paint, trim, punch list.** The last phase always expands. Everything looks worse during the work than it will once it's done. The punch list — the snag list — is that final pass where you go through the room with your checklist and catch the things that didn't land right the first time: a misaligned cabinet door, a tile grout line that needs attention, a scuff mark on the new counter.

This is where [our renovation checklist](/blog/renovation-checklist-printable/) comes in handy: going through the room systematically at the end so you're not relying on memory.

## What makes a kitchen renovation run long

Every kitchen renovation runs long by at least a little. The question is by how much. The usual suspects:

**Cabinet delays.** The single biggest source of delay on most projects, and the hardest to control. Custom cabinets take eight to twelve weeks from order to install. Semi-custom is six to eight weeks. Even stock cabinets — the "ready in a week" kind — can get delayed by shipping issues, factory backlogs, or a single wrong measurement that means the whole order has to be remade. **Order cabinets during the design phase, not after demo starts.**

**Unexpected conditions behind walls.** This isn't bad luck; it's physics. Old houses hide things. Water damage behind the sink. Knob-and-tube wiring the previous owner installed in 1952 — and if that turns out to run through the whole house, you are suddenly asking [how long it takes to rewire a house](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-rewire-a-house/) rather than how long the kitchen takes. A load-bearing wall that wasn't marked on any plan. These only reveal themselves once the drywall comes down. The fix is simple: budget a contingency for surprises, both in time and money, and don't order materials until you've torn into the walls and seen what's there. This is exactly why keeping a running [renovation budget](/blog/how-to-budget-a-home-renovation/) with a healthy contingency built in matters — because the unexpected always arrives, and the question is whether you have a plan for it or not.

**Permit and inspection delays.** Some municipalities move fast; others take weeks to schedule an inspection, and a failed inspection adds another cycle. The rough-in phase (electrical and plumbing behind the walls) needs to pass inspection before the walls can close. If the inspector shows up and finds something wrong — and inspectors *always* find something — you're looking at rework and another appointment.

**Design changes mid-project.** Someone decides, three weeks into demolition, that they want to move the sink to the opposite wall. The cabinets are already ordered. The plumber has already run the lines. Now everything gets torn out and redone. **Lock every decision before the first demo day.** If you're unsure about something, decide during the planning phase, not when the trades are waiting on the floor.

**The gap between trades.** An electrician won't start until the plumber is done with their lines. A cabinet installer won't start until the countertop template is taken. A painter won't start until every other trade has left. These gaps are invisible on paper but real on the calendar. They're also the hardest to compress.

The gap problem is why keeping a [renovation checklist](/blog/renovation-checklist-printable/) and a shared project board helps: when everyone knows what's coming next and what's blocking them, the gaps shrink by a day or two here and there — enough to matter.

## Can you speed it up?

Not really — and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. What you *can* do is reduce the probability of delay by being methodical about the things you control:

1. **Lock your design before demo.** Every decision — layout, cabinet style, fixture locations, electrical points — should be finalised and, if possible, drawn up before the first wall comes down.

2. **Order long-lead items during the design phase.** Cabinets first. Then major appliances if they constrain the layout. Everything else can wait.

3. **Build buffer weeks into your timeline.** Plan for the longer end of the range. If you budget twelve weeks and finish in ten, that's a win. If you plan for eight and something goes wrong, you're in a different relationship with the project every day.

4. **Get permits approved before you start paying trades.** Nothing derails a schedule like an unpermitted renovation getting flagged partway through.

5. **Do a walk-through of the existing kitchen with a contractor before committing to a timeline.** They'll see the water damage, the outdated wiring, the load-bearing wall that wasn't on the plans. They can give you a more grounded estimate than a website can.

6. **Track what's actually happening on site.** Not just costs — the decisions, the photos of every stage, the sequence of work. When you're managing a renovation, a [photo and note log](/blog/what-to-track-during-a-renovation/) is worth its weight in saved arguments. You'll thank yourself three weeks later when the electrician asks whether the wiring was already moved.

## The short answer

A straightforward kitchen refresh takes four to eight weeks on site, all else being equal. A full gut renovation with moved walls and new fixtures takes ten to sixteen weeks. Add four to eight weeks for design and permits before the work starts. That puts most residential kitchen renovations somewhere in the range of three to six months from the first sketch to the final walkthrough.

The spread inside that range isn't random. It's determined by how much you move, how old the house is, and whether you order your cabinets before the demo crew walks in.
