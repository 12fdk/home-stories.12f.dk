There's a moment in nearly every insurance claim after a renovation that decides the outcome, and it comes long after the plaster has dried. The adjuster or claims handler wants to know what changed in the house, what it cost, and that the work was done properly. If you kept a record, that conversation takes a day. If you didn't, it takes months of reconstructing a project from memory, faded thermal receipts, and a camera roll where the photo of the open wall is buried under four hundred selfies.

The good news is that the record nobody has is the one that takes the least effort to build — if you start it before the first trade walks in. This post is the version I wish I'd read first: what to document, when to document it, and what a proper trail actually looks like when someone official asks to see it.

## Start with the before, not the after

The most common documentation habit — and the most useless — is photographing the finished result. The finished room proves nothing to anyone: it proves you painted the kitchen, not that the floor beneath it is sound, or that the wiring was pulled properly before the boards went up.

The valuable baseline is the *before*. Photograph every space you'll touch, in its pre-renovation state, the week work starts. Cracked tile, water-stained ceiling, the old boiler with its serial plate visible. Why does that matter? Because when a claim later touches one of those spaces, the before photo is the evidence that the damage you're claiming appeared after your work began — and the evidence that the damage you're *not* claiming was already there. Insurers and, in a bad case, lawyers, live in that gap between "was it like that when we started" and "what does it look like now."

This is the same instinct as [keeping pre-work photos of every wall before it closes](/blog/what-to-track-during-a-renovation/), and it applies here for the same reason: the photo you take in five minutes now is the one that answers a question in three months that would otherwise cost real money to find out.

## What the money trail has to show

A claims file that's strong on the money side answers four questions without anyone asking twice:

- **What was the work, and who did it?** The signed quote or work order, with the contractor's name, the scope in their own words, and a date. Not the text-message version of it — the paper one.
- **What was it actually?** The final invoice, itemized where possible. The one that matches the quote, or the one that explains why it doesn't (more on change orders below).
- **What was paid, and when?** A bank statement or card history that lines up with the invoices. Insurers check that the money left the account; a stack of invoices without a matching payment pattern reads as a reconstruction, not a record.
- **What did the materials cost, and where?** Receipts for fixtures, fittings, and any supplies you bought yourself — the [photo-first receipt habit](/blog/how-to-organize-renovation-receipts/) is the one with the highest payoff-per-second in the whole project.

The last point is the one people skip most. The big invoices for the trades are kept in a folder; the hardware-store receipts for the two thousand dollars of fixtures, plumbing, and tiles are in a drawer with the batteries. A claim that covers the full scope of a project needs the whole picture, not just the big ticket. If you're budgeting the project in the first place, keep the [budget line items](/blog/how-to-budget-a-home-renovation/) and the receipts in the same structure — the budget is the prediction, the receipts are the proof it happened.

## The middle of the project is where the evidence lives

The "before" and the "after" get the attention. The middle is where a claim is actually won or lost, because it's where the work that isn't visible anymore happened.

When a wall is open, photograph it. Cables, pipes, junction boxes, the way the new drainage run connects to the old — all of it goes behind plasterboard, and from that day forward, every question about it is a question that costs money to answer, and in a claim, a question that can't be answered is a claim that gets discounted. Shoot wide for context and close for anything that carries water or current. Let the phone stamp the date; don't rename the files.

The same applies to anything that's installed and then concealed: insulation in a wall or attic, a new tank in the loft, underfloor heating in a screed. The photo of the installation, taken the day it happened, is the only proof it was installed at all, let alone installed to the standard you were promised.

A small habit makes this painless: one folder (or one album) per project, one sub-folder per room, shot chronologically. When the claim comes, you're sending a coherent folder instead of explaining why the photo of the bathroom is in a group chat you can't remember the name of.

## Change orders and scope creep: the document most people don't have

Here's the situation that shows up again and again, and it's the one a documentation habit quietly prevents. The work starts. Three weeks in, the contractor says the wall's in worse shape than expected and the price is going up. You agree, in the hallway, over coffee. Six weeks later the final invoice is a few thousand more than the quote you signed, and when the claim is made, the adjuster asks for the paperwork that authorizes the increase.

There isn't any. There's your memory and the contractor's, and those two memories are now, understandably, different.

The fix is the cheapest one in this entire post: **no money moves without a line of writing.** It can be an email, a message, a signed addendum — anything with a date that says what changed and by how much. The mechanics of dealing with change orders are their own subject ([managing contractor change orders](/blog/managing-contractor-change-orders/) covers it properly), but the documentation side is one rule: if it's not written down, it's a conversation, and conversations don't survive a claims file.

This is also where a contingency budget starts to feel like insurance. If you set aside a [contingency for the surprises](/blog/renovation-contingency-budget/) from the start, every change order is a line item you can trace to a decision, and the whole money trail stays coherent. If you didn't, the final invoice has a gap in it that nobody can explain — and gaps in a claims file get read against you.

## The one-page summary: the document that does the work

If you keep only one thing from this post, make it the project summary. One page, written in the last week of the project, that says:

- What the project was and when it ran.
- Room by room, what changed.
- Who did each job, with the date and the price.
- The total cost, and how it relates to the original budget.
- A list of the photo folders, so anyone can find the before/during/after for any room.

That page is what you hand to the claims handler first. It's also what you hand to a buyer when you sell, what you use to work out what's covered when the water stops coming, and what saves you an hour of scrolling through a camera roll on a Sunday evening when you're trying to remember which plumber did the bathroom.

Twenty minutes to write. The alternative is an afternoon of reconstruction, and usually the reconstruction isn't as good as the record would have been.

## The habits, in one list

None of this takes equipment. A phone, a folder, and a minute at the right moments:

1. **Before work starts:** photograph every space in its current state.
2. **At the till:** photograph receipts as you get them. Don't wait for the shoebox.
3. **When a wall opens:** photograph what's inside, wide and close.
4. **When something's concealed:** photograph the installation the day it happens.
5. **When the price changes:** get it in writing the day it changes.
6. **At the end:** write the one-page summary.

Do all six and you have a record that answers the insurer's questions before they're asked. Do none, and the claim becomes a reconstruction from memory — and memory is the one thing you can't photograph.
